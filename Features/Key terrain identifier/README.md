ElevationSampler + curvature analysis
Automatically finds dominant ground, natural choke points, re-entrants, and inter-visibility lines from raw DEM — the terrain features that control movement and observation.
Decision supported: Which ground to seize, hold, or deny first. Avenues of approach the enemy will use regardless of intent.


The four-stage pipeline
The whole analysis is a pipeline: sample → smooth → detect → score. Each stage has a specific job and they compose cleanly.
Stage 1 — Gaussian smooth before anything else. The raw DEM has per-pixel noise — a 1–2 m elevation error at a single cell creates a local maximum that looks like a terrain feature but is just sensor noise. The 5×5 Gaussian kernel (σ=1.0) removes that noise while preserving features larger than ~3 cells. Without this, the curvature computation amplifies noise into thousands of false peaks. With it, only genuine terrain structure survives.
Stage 2 — Laplacian curvature, the right operator for this problem. The Laplacian ∇²f = (N + S + E + W − 4C) / h² is the second spatial derivative of elevation. It is positive at convex terrain (hilltops, ridges — terrain curves away from you in all directions) and negative at concave terrain (valleys, re-entrants — terrain curves toward you). This is exactly the mathematical signature of the feature types you care about. The plan curvature separates elongated ridges (high plan curvature) from round hilltops (low plan curvature), which determines whether a feature gets classified as dominant_ground or ridge. The curvature heatmap overlay in red/blue is the raw output of this computation — red is convex terrain an enemy will defend, blue is concave terrain they will use to approach.
Stage 3 — Topographic prominence, not just elevation. A hilltop at 800 m on a plateau that sits at 780 m has 20 m of prominence. A hilltop at 600 m that rises from a 400 m valley has 200 m of prominence. The second feature is operationally more significant even though it is lower in absolute terms — it dominates everything around it. Prominence is computed by sampling a ring of cells at radius R from each candidate and measuring how far above the ring minimum the candidate sits. This avoids the naive "highest elevation wins" ranking that puts useless flat plateau corners at the top of the list.
Stage 4 — Per-peak viewshed scoring, the tactically decisive component. After finding candidate features geometrically, each one gets a fast viewshed computed from its summit. The horizon-sweep algorithm (same as the Dead Ground Mapper) casts 36 rays at 10° intervals, counts visible cells, and produces a viewshedPct — how much of the analysis area that feature can observe. A dominant ground feature with 70% viewshed scores dramatically higher than one with 30%. This is what separates terrain that controls the ground from terrain that merely occupies it. The composite score weights viewshed at 40% — the largest single factor — because observation is what makes terrain key rather than just high.
The composite score and its weights. Prominence 35% + viewshed 40% + elevation 15% + type weight 10%. The type weight is lowest because it is the least objective — a re-entrant is inherently less tactically valuable than dominant ground, but a very prominent re-entrant with good observation of the approach can outrank a mediocre hilltop. The weights keep the score objective while allowing type to break ties. The score is out of 100 and displayed on each card alongside the three component bars so a planner can see at a glance why a feature ranked where it did.
The saddle detection is the most tactically underappreciated output. Saddles are local minima in one axis and local maxima in the other — the lowest point between two higher features. They score well not because they have high viewshed but because they control movement. Anything moving through that terrain mass will cross at or near the saddle. A blocking position at a saddle forces a detour of potentially kilometres. The assessment text makes this explicit rather than leaving interpretation to the map reader.
The ranked list panel is the deliverable. The 3D markers tell you where to look. The list tells you what to do about it. Each card has a one-line tactical assessment that could be read verbatim at an O-group — "Controls 68% of AO. Seizure gives observation and fires over all lower ground. Must be held or denied." That text is computed, not generic.
